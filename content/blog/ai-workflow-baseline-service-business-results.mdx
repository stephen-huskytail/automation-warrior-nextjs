An **AI workflow baseline** is the short evidence packet a service-business leader prepares before changing a recurring process with AI. It captures the current version of the work in enough detail to answer a practical question later: did the new method make this service step better, or did it merely move effort and risk somewhere harder to see?

That is different from an AI dashboard. A dashboard summarizes ongoing signals. A baseline preserves the starting conditions for one bounded process: the handoff map, the definition of complete, a small sample of real cases, the assumptions behind the timing, and the change log for the test. It gives a team something concrete to compare when early results are enthusiastic, mixed, or unexpectedly costly.

> **Quick Summary**
>
> - Build an AI workflow baseline as a small evidence packet for one recurring service process.
> - Capture the handoff map, completion definition, timing anchor, representative-case sample, and assumptions before changing the method.
> - Use a measurement dictionary so everyone records the same event in the same way.
> - Keep a change log; a comparison is weak when the workflow, team, source system, or case mix changes without a record.
> - Treat the baseline as decision support, not proof of a promised productivity, revenue, safety, or compliance outcome.

## Build the AI workflow baseline packet first

Start with a process that a team can point to, rather than a broad aspiration such as “use AI in client service.” A good candidate has a familiar trigger, a defined recipient, and an observable handoff. For example: preparing an internal account brief after a project update, organizing a new intake packet for review, or assembling a delivery-status summary for a manager.

Create one baseline packet with five parts. It can live in a simple shared document or spreadsheet; it does not need a new analytics program.

1. **Workflow card.** State the trigger, the person who starts the work, approved inputs, expected output, reviewer, receiving role, and the point at which the current process is complete.
2. **Handoff map.** Draw the actual sequence of people and systems involved. Include the places where someone looks for information, waits for an answer, checks a draft, or carries unresolved work forward.
3. **Measurement dictionary.** Define the few observations you will collect. If “complete” means “manager accepts the internal brief without returning it,” write that exact definition. If a case is excluded because it contains incomplete source material, define that too.
4. **Case sample.** Gather a small set of recent cases that resembles the work the team expects to test. Include routine work and a few cases where the normal path was interrupted.
5. **Change log.** Record every meaningful change during the test: a new template, different reviewer, revised source, new automation, training session, or altered completion rule.

The packet makes a [focused AI strategy](/strategy) more operational. Instead of debating a general promise about AI, the owner can decide whether a named workflow deserves a bounded test, what must remain under review, and what evidence would justify the next step.

## Choose one timing anchor people can reproduce

Teams often disagree about timing because they are silently measuring different events. One person starts the clock when a request arrives. Another starts when the information is ready. A third stops when a draft exists, while the recipient cares about when the handoff is usable.

Pick one start event and one finish event for the baseline. Write them down before collecting a sample. For an internal account brief, the start might be “all approved project notes are available in the designated location.” The finish might be “the manager accepts the brief or returns it with a recorded correction.” That definition does not make every case identical, but it makes the comparison intelligible.

Then record two separate observations where relevant:

- **Elapsed time:** the time from the agreed start event to the agreed finish event; and
- **touch time:** the approximate active minutes spent by the people completing and checking the work.

Those measures answer different questions. A process can have low touch time but long elapsed time because it waits for a source or approver. It can also move quickly through the queue while consuming a great deal of senior-review time. Keeping the two observations separate prevents a short generation step from being presented as a complete operating result.

## Write a measurement dictionary, not a vague scorecard

A measurement dictionary is simply a set of plain-language definitions. Its job is to prevent the team from changing a term after it sees the first results. Keep it brief and specific to the process.

For a service-workflow baseline, it can include these fields:

- **Eligible case:** a request that has the agreed source materials and falls within the workflow’s approved purpose.
- **Completion state:** accepted, returned for correction, redirected to a specialist, paused, or completed through the existing manual path.
- **Material correction:** a change that alters a fact, required detail, handoff, customer commitment, or decision—not a cosmetic edit.
- **Source issue:** missing, conflicting, outdated, inaccessible, or not approved for this workflow.
- **Exception:** a case that cannot follow the normal path without a named decision, additional expertise, or a manual fallback.
- **Context note:** a changed condition, such as an unusual volume spike, staff absence, new client requirement, or source-system outage.

Do not turn the dictionary into a universal AI policy. It is a shared vocabulary for one test. The more accurately the labels describe the work, the easier it is for the team to see whether a problem belongs in the source material, process design, training, review step, or the proposed tool.

## Capture a representative case sample

A baseline sample should be deliberately useful, not statistically theatrical. Select recent completed cases from a normal operating period where possible. Avoid a sample made only of easy wins, emergencies, or unusual high-stakes work. If the team expects the test to serve multiple kinds of requests, label the kinds rather than blending them into one average.

For every case, capture only what the measurement dictionary requires. A simple baseline row might include the workflow type, start and finish events, active roles, completion state, material corrections, source issues, exception status, and a short context note. Use non-sensitive identifiers or anonymized labels when appropriate; do not create a new repository of client information merely to evaluate a process.

After the rows are complete, conduct a 20-minute walkthrough with someone who performs the work and someone who receives it. Ask where the record fails to describe the real effort. Did the team omit time spent chasing an approved source? Did a reviewer make an invisible but essential correction? Did staff routinely route a class of requests around the process? Update the baseline definitions before introducing the AI-assisted method.

This walkthrough is a practical version of the evidence discipline behind [AI peer review for service teams](/blog/ai-peer-review-service-teams): bring the people closest to the work into the review, use representative examples, and make uncertainty visible rather than rewarding the most polished output.

## Preserve the starting conditions with a change log

A comparison becomes unreliable when the baseline and the test no longer describe the same process. Teams naturally change things while learning: they improve the source template, clarify an intake field, train a reviewer, replace a document store, or narrow the cases allowed into the pilot. These can be good changes. They simply need to be recorded.

For each change, log the date, what changed, why it changed, who approved it, and which cases it may affect. This creates three useful options at the next review:

- compare only cases completed before and after the change point;
- run the updated method long enough to establish a fresh comparison period; or
- decide that the workflow changed too much for the original baseline to answer the question.

The discipline also helps a leader avoid accidental scope expansion. A workflow that begins as internal preparation can become a customer-facing recommendation, a durable system update, or a new data-integration path. Those are not minor adjustments to the same test. They may require new ownership, different expertise, or a separate baseline.

## Use the baseline at the pilot decision meeting

At the first review, place the original packet beside the current sample. Do not ask whether the tool is impressive. Ask whether the process changed in the way the team intended and whether the evidence is complete enough for a decision.

A useful meeting can work through four prompts:

1. Which completion states became more or less common?
2. Did the handoff become clearer for the receiving role, or did review and correction simply move to a different person?
3. Which source issues and exceptions persisted, and which were introduced by the new method?
4. Has any change in people, volume, policy, or source quality made the comparison less reliable?

The [AI pilot scale decision](/blog/ai-pilot-scale-decision-service-businesses) is a useful next step once the baseline and test period are documented. The result might support a limited expansion, a narrowed use case, a revised workflow, or a return to the existing method. Each can be a responsible outcome when it follows the evidence rather than a preselected success story.

## Pros and cons of a baseline packet

The benefit is that a baseline packet is light enough to use while preserving the reality of the work. It gives leaders a clear initial state, lets reviewers point to specific handoffs and corrections, and makes changed conditions visible. It can prevent a demo from becoming an unexamined operating commitment.

The limitation is that a small sample will not answer every question. Teams may estimate touch time, miss a context note, or find that their original completion definition is too loose. The packet also takes time from people who know the workflow. Keep it proportionate: improve the definitions when they create confusion, but do not turn a pilot into a reporting burden that displaces the service work itself.

The NIST [AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) provides voluntary guidance for organizations considering AI-related risks. Its [Generative AI Profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf) includes risk considerations and suggested actions for generative AI. These references can inform how a team frames its questions, but they do not certify a workflow, replace specialist review, or establish a legal, security, privacy, compliance, financial, or professional conclusion.

## Experience, expertise, and limits (E-E-A-T)

This article provides an operating method for service-business leaders assessing a proposed AI-assisted process. It does not claim that AI will reduce costs, increase revenue, improve quality, or be appropriate for every workflow. It also does not offer legal, privacy, security, compliance, financial, employment, or domain advice.

The appropriate level of review depends on the process, data, recipients, customer commitments, and consequences of error. Work involving sensitive information, regulated decisions, safety concerns, or material consequences may need technical, security, privacy, legal, compliance, or domain specialists before a team tests or expands an AI-assisted method. Keep people accountable for consequential decisions and retain a manual path where it is needed.

## Start with one visible starting point

Choose one recurring service process and build its baseline packet before changing the workflow. Map the handoffs, define the timing anchor and completion state, gather a small representative sample, and record every material change during the test. That gives the team an honest starting point for the decision that follows.
