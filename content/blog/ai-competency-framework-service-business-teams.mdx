An **AI competency framework** gives a service business a practical way to define the judgment people need before an AI-assisted workflow becomes routine. It is not a scorecard for who has tried the newest tool. It is a shared standard for using approved information, checking work, handling exceptions, and improving the method while people remain accountable for the outcome.

For leaders, the value is clarity. A team may have broad access to AI and still lack a reliable way to prepare a client update, summarize an internal case, or organize an intake packet. A framework makes the required capabilities visible in the context of a real workflow. It also makes a more useful question possible: what would a person need to demonstrate before this work can be used with less supervision?

> **Quick Summary**
>
> - An AI competency framework should describe observable judgment in a real service workflow—not tool familiarity alone.
> - Start with a small, repeatable task and define the approved sources, completion standard, reviewer, and escalation path.
> - Assess people with representative cases, including an ordinary case, a missing-information case, and an exception.
> - Use coaching and peer review to improve the workflow as well as the individual.
> - Measure completed-work quality, total effort, adoption, and the intended business result before expanding use.

## What an AI competency framework should measure

A useful AI competency framework measures whether someone can complete a bounded task with appropriate judgment. It should not reward a person for producing the most text, using the most features, or adopting a particular prompt style. Those signals can be interesting, but they do not show that the next person received accurate, usable work.

For a service business, capability normally combines five connected practices:

1. **Frame the work.** The person can identify the task, intended audience, outcome, and boundary. They know whether the workflow prepares a draft, supports an internal decision, or reaches a client-facing handoff.
2. **Use approved sources.** The person can locate the information the workflow is allowed to use, recognize when a source is missing or conflicting, and avoid filling gaps with an unsupported assumption.
3. **Review the output.** The person can compare the result with the source, identify material errors or omissions, and decide whether the output meets the agreed completion standard.
4. **Handle exceptions.** The person can pause, use a manual fallback, or route the work to the right owner when the case does not fit the approved path.
5. **Improve responsibly.** The person can document recurring corrections and help refine the instructions or workflow without turning a one-off workaround into an unreviewed rule.

These practices align capability with the business process rather than the interface of one model or vendor. They also create a concrete starting point for an [AI strategy](/strategy): leaders can connect a skill expectation to a priority, a workflow owner, and an outcome worth measuring.

## Start with one recognizable service workflow

Choose work that occurs often enough to practice, has a visible beginning and end, and can be reviewed without creating unnecessary customer risk. Examples may include preparing an internal account brief from approved notes, organizing intake details for a human reviewer, drafting a delivery-status update for review, or assembling a routine project handoff.

The right first workflow is not necessarily the task that looks most impressive in a demo. It is the task where the team can define what good work looks like. Before using it as a competency exercise, document:

- the trigger and the expected completed-work result;
- the sources a person may use and the sources they must not use;
- the facts or decisions that require a check;
- the reviewer or owner who can accept the work;
- the conditions that require a pause, escalation, or manual completion.

This baseline prevents a common error: treating a polished draft as a completed service outcome. A response can sound convincing while omitting a source, misunderstanding a client detail, or making a recommendation outside the brief. The [AI implementation checklist](/blog/ai-implementation-checklist-service-workflows) offers a companion structure for defining the workflow, review point, and fallback before a wider rollout.

## Define proficiency with observable behaviors

A framework becomes usable when each level describes behavior a manager or peer can actually observe. Avoid labels such as “beginner” or “expert” unless they explain what someone can do differently in the work.

A simple three-level model can be enough for an early pilot:

**Guided practitioner.** The person can perform the workflow with an approved template, identify the required sources, and ask for help when information is missing. They do not independently expand the use case or accept work outside the defined boundary.

**Independent practitioner.** The person can complete ordinary cases, review the output against the agreed checks, explain the source support for important statements, and use the escalation path for exceptions. They can distinguish a useful draft from a final, accountable decision.

**Workflow contributor.** The person can recognize recurring failure patterns, bring representative evidence to the owner, and help test a bounded improvement. This level does not give someone authority to change policy, approve a consequential action, or bypass a specialist. It means they can contribute informed feedback to the operating method.

For role-specific detail, an [AI skills matrix for service teams](/blog/ai-skills-matrix-service-teams) can map the judgment, verification, escalation, and coaching expectations to the people who actually prepare, review, and receive the work. Keep the rubric short enough that it can be used in a real review, not only a leadership presentation.

## Practice on representative cases, not ideal demos

Use a small set of approved examples that mirrors the work people will encounter. Include one ordinary case, one case with incomplete or conflicting information, and one exception that should result in a pause or escalation. Redact or protect sensitive information as appropriate for the environment.

Ask the person completing the exercise to show their reasoning in practical terms: which approved sources they used, what they verified, what they changed, what remains uncertain, and what they would do next. This is more revealing than asking whether they liked the tool.

Then review the work against the same standard for everyone. A good review asks:

- Is the output supported by the allowed sources?
- Is it complete enough for the named recipient and intended purpose?
- Did the person identify a material uncertainty or exception?
- Did they keep the result within the workflow boundary?
- Is the manual fallback clear if the result cannot be used?

A peer-review loop can make that practice more consistent. The [AI peer review guide](/blog/ai-peer-review-service-teams) explains how a colleague can help catch weak outputs and surface patterns without creating a second approval ritual for every low-risk task. The aim is shared judgment, not a contest over whose prompt is best.

## Build coaching into the operating rhythm

Competency is not a one-time training event. It develops when people receive feedback on completed work, see how an exception was resolved, and revisit the standard as the workflow changes. A short recurring review can be enough: look at a few representative cases, name one recurring correction, decide whether it is a people, process, source, or tool issue, and assign the next experiment to an accountable owner.

The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) is a voluntary resource intended to help organizations incorporate trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI products, services, and systems. Its related generative-AI profile can inform questions about risk and oversight. It is not a certification, a guarantee that a workflow is safe, or a substitute for technical, security, privacy, legal, compliance, or domain-specific advice.

That distinction matters in coaching. A service team should not be told to “fix the prompt” when the actual issue is an unclear policy, inaccessible source, missing owner, or task that should remain manual. An [AI exception escalation runbook](/blog/ai-exception-escalation-service-teams) gives people a practical route when a case no longer fits the approved method.

## Measure whether capability improves the work

Training attendance, logins, and generated-output counts do not establish that a team is becoming more capable. Track a small set of comparable cases over time and review the complete operating picture:

- **Completed-work quality:** Was the next person able to use the result without avoidable correction or rework?
- **Adoption:** Did eligible people choose the workflow when it fit, and what stopped them when they did not?
- **Total effort:** What changed in preparation, review, correction, exception handling, and maintenance—not only first-draft time?
- **Business result:** Did the workflow support the defined priority, such as more consistent delivery, faster response, or better follow-up?

This avoids claiming that recovered minutes are automatically cost savings or revenue. The [AI operations scorecard](/blog/ai-operations-scorecard-measure-what-matters) explains how quality, adoption, effort, risk, and outcomes can be considered together. The evidence may show that the workflow needs narrower use, better source preparation, or a different owner. That is useful learning, not a failed measurement exercise.

## Pros and cons of a competency framework

The main benefit is that a framework turns vague expectations into a shared, reviewable standard. It can reduce inconsistent handoffs, give managers a more constructive coaching conversation, and make it easier to see whether a pilot has a real operating path. It also makes it harder to confuse tool access with reliable capability.

The tradeoffs are real. Building a good rubric takes time from people who understand the work. Overly broad criteria can become a bureaucratic checklist, while overly rigid criteria can prevent teams from flagging a legitimate exception. Some workflows cannot safely be used as an early practice environment because of sensitive information, regulated decisions, customer commitments, or unclear ownership. In those cases, the right next step may be a process, security, privacy, legal, compliance, or domain review—not more training.

## Experience, expertise, and limits (E-E-A-T)

This article provides an operating framework for leaders of service businesses considering how to build team capability around AI-assisted work. It does not promise a specific productivity, financial, quality, or adoption outcome. Results depend on the chosen workflow, the quality and availability of approved information, team capacity, review practices, implementation decisions, and the consequences of errors.

A responsible framework keeps people accountable for consequential decisions and customer commitments. It uses representative evidence instead of a polished demo, names the limits of an assisted workflow, and brings in the appropriate specialists when the work requires them. The NIST resource cited above is a voluntary reference, not an assurance that a particular use case meets any legal, regulatory, security, or contractual obligation.

## Put the framework to work

Choose one recurring workflow and define three examples: an ordinary case, a missing-information case, and an exception. Name the owner, the allowed sources, the completion check, and the manual fallback. That gives a team a credible first capability standard without pretending every role needs the same level of access or autonomy.
